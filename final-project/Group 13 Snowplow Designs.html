00 · Short version

What I would build

Phase 1

Sonar turret on your current chassis. Stop at each tile centre, look left, ahead and right, and record walls. Start coding this week with the spin-scan version, which needs no new parts.

Phase 2

Roller sweeper with a tilting hopper, sized with the checker below: intake ≤ 25 cm wide, front ≤ 11 cm ahead of the axle, 4 cm chamfers. Keep the bucket loader as the simpler backup.

Software

Tile-by-tile explorer, one control loop, map saved as JSON. Phase 2 reloads the map and replays it. Your lab 3 line correction carries over almost unchanged, because every street tile boundary is a black line.

01 · The brief

The task as I understand it

  • Board: 5 × 9 tiles, possibly two tables joined (10 × 9 or 5 × 18). The handouts use 30.48 cm tiles, so keep TILE_SIZE in one config value.
  • Street: one tile wide, lined on both sides by 30 × 30 × 30 cm cubes. Inside the street, the floor still has the black grid lines, so every tile boundary you cross is a correction opportunity.
  • Phase 1: find the street from S1 and S2, drive it and map it. Touching houses is allowed.
  • Phase 2: collect 50 pom-poms and release them at the drop location. No touching houses. Pom-poms must be carried, not dragged along the floor.
  • Hardware: phase 1 and phase 2 may use different hardware, so the intake does not need to exist in phase 1.
  • Materials: the kit, plus paper, cardboard and tape.

Your robot today

  • Wheel radius 2.1 cm and track width 12.0 cm, calibrated in lab 2. Line sensors sit 6.0 cm ahead of the axle.
  • At 150 dps it drives 5.5 cm/s, about 5.5 s per tile. A 10-tile street is about a minute of driving before turns and scans.
  • From the photo: compact two-wheel base, BrickPi on stacked beam layers, colour sensor low beside the wheel, caster at the back. Loose cables hang outside the footprint; tape them in before phase 1 so they cannot catch on a house.
  • Measure the farthest point from the axle centre. My estimate is ~13 cm, which lets it spin in place in any tile.

What pom-poms are like

  • Light. Bulk packs run about 200–250 per pound, so ~2 g each and ~100 g for all 50. Weight never matters.
  • Smaller than first assumed. Your test pom-poms look about 1.3–1.5 cm and black, so 50 of them take only ~0.13 L loose. A mixed ½″–1½″ pack would be ~0.9 L. See Roller v2.
  • Grippy when squeezed, dead when pushed. They don't roll like balls. Rollers and flaps that pinch them work well. Ramps that rely on them rolling don't.
  • Dark ones look like lines. A black or navy pom-pom under a colour sensor reads like a grid line. Put the line sensors behind the intake.
03 · Street geometry

The 90° turn decides the intake size

Driving straight, a robot only needs to be narrower than the street. Turning at a corner is the hard part, because a wide intake swings out toward the house on the far side. The checker below sweeps your footprint through a 90° turn and finds the best place to stop before turning. Set it to your measured dimensions.

Rule of thumb

Turn radius (axle centre to farthest point) under ~14.5 cm can turn at any corner. Over ~16 cm you need tricks: stop early, raise the tool, or do a three-point turn. Chamfering the intake's front corners is the cheapest way to shrink the radius without losing width.

Edge coverage

A single pass down the centre misses pom-poms hugging the houses. Assuming even placement and 2 cm pom-poms, a 25 cm intake misses about 5 of 50, 24 cm misses about 7, and 22 cm misses about 11. A second pass in offset lanes, or edge spinners (P2-D), closes the gap.

04 · Phase 1 hardware

Exploring the street

Phase 1 needs one answer per tile: is there a house to the left, ahead and to the right? Pose comes from the odometry and line correction you already have. In a straight tile the side houses are 15 cm from the centreline, so a sonar at the tile centre reads about 10–12 cm for a wall and 45 cm or more (often 255) for an opening. Use a threshold around 28 cm.

05 · Phase 2 hardware

Collecting the snow

Phase 2 needs a mechanism that lifts pom-poms off the floor into something the robot carries, holds about a litre, fits the checker limits and lets go at the drop point. That takes two mechanism motors next to the two drive motors, so ports A and D are both used. Line sensors stay, which leaves one sensor port for the gyro and one for the ultrasonic sensor or a limit switch.

P2-A

Roller sweeper with tilting hopper

Recommended

Update after the prototype: see Roller v2. The roller now drops pom-poms into a low tray instead of lifting them to a hopper.

Roller: a paper-towel tube with 6–8 paper or thin-card flaps taped along it, on a LEGO axle through card end plugs. Drive it from the medium motor and gear it up 2–3× with the kit gears. At 250 rpm direct the flap tips move ~0.8 m/s, which only flings a pom-pom ~3 cm up. Faster clears the ramp. Let the flaps just brush the ramp.

Ramp and hood: card ramp taped flat to a thin lip (~2 mm), side walls from the white panels, and a card hood over the roller so nothing is thrown forward. Hopper: ~10 × 18 × 7 cm card box over the axle, hinged at the back. The NXT motor tilts it to dump behind the robot, so reverse up to the drop point.

05b · Roller v2 · after the Nov 2 prototype

Why the prototype missed, and a version that shouldn't

Your prototype was close to what I drew, but the drawing hid the part that matters most: the roller, the curved guide under it and the frame holding both must be one rigid unit, so the gap between flap tips and guide stays the same everywhere. Your test pom-poms also look about 1.3–1.5 cm across (judging by the LEGO beams beside them), smaller than the 1–4 cm I assumed. That makes every clearance tighter.

The key change: split the job

Don't ask the roller to lift pom-poms 7 cm into a hopper. Like a carpet sweeper, it only needs to move each one about 1.5 cm up and back into a low tray, guided the whole way. The big lift happens once, at the drop point, with a separate slow motion: the tray goes on an arm (the P2-C arm idea).

Build it in this order

  1. Cut two side plates from corrugated cardboard using the full-size template. Push a LEGO beam through each at the axle mark, or glue a beam on, so the axle runs in a LEGO hole instead of in cardboard.
  2. Make the hub: roll cardstock tightly around a long axle to ~3 cm across, with card or LEGO plugs at each end so it can't slip. Tape it hard. It must be straight and round.
  3. Tape on 8 flap strips evenly (every 45°), full length, tabs pointing the same way. Slit the free edge every 3 cm.
  4. Bend the guide: a 23 × 4 cm cardstock strip, glued to the arc line on both side plates so it follows the tips. Tape the skirt to its front edge, flat on the table.
  5. Check the gap by hand: spin the rotor and slide a LEGO beam between tips and guide at the left, middle and right. It should just brush everywhere.
  6. Add the hood and tray, then mount the drive gear outside one side plate.

Bench test before mounting

Hold the module and slide it along a ruler at ~3 cm/s (about 10 cm every 3 seconds) with the rotor running. Lay 20 pom-poms across its path: 4 at each end within 1 cm of the side plates, the rest spread out, including two touching pairs.

Record each one as caught, pushed, thrown or jammed. Change one setting at a time: gap first, then speed, then flap stiffness. Aim for 19 of 20 caught three runs in a row before you put it on the robot.

Black pom-poms

They read exactly like grid lines. With the tray in front of the wheels, move the line sensors behind the axle. Your snapping code works unchanged with a negative SENSOR_OFFSET (about −5 cm).

Coordinates and the entry

Keep the lab 2 and 3 convention: θ = 0 is +y and 90 is +x, with distances in cm. Tiles are named by their lower-left intersection, so tile (i, j) has its centre at ((i + ½)·T, (j + ½)·T). S1 and S2 are adjacent intersections. The two tiles sharing that edge are the candidates for the first street tile, and the street is the one with houses on both sides. Check both with one scan before committing.

TILE = 30.48                    # 30.0 if the course tiles are 30 cm
HEAD = {"N": 0.0, "E": 90.0, "S": 180.0, "W": 270.0}
STEP = {"N": (0, 1), "E": (1, 0), "S": (0, -1), "W": (-1, 0)}
LEFT = {"N": "W", "W": "S", "S": "E", "E": "N"}
RIGHT = {v: k for k, v in LEFT.items()}

def tile_centre(i, j):
    return ((i + 0.5) * TILE, (j + 0.5) * TILE)

def entry_candidates(s1, s2):
    (x1, y1), (x2, y2) = sorted([s1, s2])
    if y1 == y2:                         # horizontal edge
        return (x1, y1), (x1, y1 - 1)    # tile above, tile below
    return (x1, y1), (x1 - 1, y1)        # tile right, tile left

Tick loop skeleton

class State:
    def step(self, r):  # r = robot snapshot for this tick
        return self      # or the next State

def run(first_state, hz=50):
    state, period = first_state, 1.0 / hz
    while state is not None:
        t0 = time.time()
        r = robot.read_all()        # encoders, colour, gyro, sonar
        odom.update(r)
        lines.update(r, odom)       # may snap x/y/theta
        state = state.step(r)
        log.write(r, odom, state)   # CSV, one row per tick
        time.sleep(max(0, period - (time.time() - t0)))
    robot.stop()

Wall scan with your filters module

from utils.filters import MedianWindow
WALL_CM = 28        # wall reads ~10–12 at tile centre

def read_cm(us, n=5):
    w = MedianWindow(n)
    for _ in range(n):
        v = us.get_cm()
        if v is not None and v > 0:
            w.append(v)
        time.sleep(0.05)
    return w.get_value() or 255

def scan(turret, us):
    walls = {}
    for side, ang in (("L", -90), ("F", 0), ("R", 90)):
        turret.aim(ang)
        walls[side] = read_cm(us) < WALL_CM
    turret.aim(0)
    return walls
Carry-overs from the lab 3 code to fix before building on it
  • get_red() or 100 hides a real zero. In Python 0 or 100 is 100, so a reading of exactly 0 on a very dark line counts as "no line". Use v if v is not None else 100.
  • The ignore window is counted in loop iterations. IGNORE_RED_CTR_START = 100 lasts a different distance at every speed. Gate by distance instead: only accept a line when odometry says one is within about ±5 cm.
  • align_on_line can spin forever. If one sensor never sees the line (a pom-pom on it, or a seam), the crawl loop has no exit. Add a distance or time limit, and fall back to a single-sensor snap.
  • update_odometer reads both colour sensors and prints on every call. That is two extra SPI reads and a console write about 100 times a second. Print at 5 Hz, and read the sensors once per tick.
  • Straight driving is open loop. Equal wheel speeds drift. Add a P term on heading error (gyro or odometry) between lines. In a 30 cm street, lateral drift is what touches houses.
  • State is global. Move pose into an Odometer object so phase 1 and phase 2 programs share one tested module.
def line_expected(pos_along, tile=TILE, window=5.0):
    """True when the sensor is within `window` cm of a grid line."""
    d = pos_along % tile
    return d < window or d > tile - window

def is_dark(v, thr=LINE_THRESHOLD):
    return v is not None and v < thr
Coverage strategy

Default to one centred pass. If misses near the houses cost points, run the street twice in lanes offset by ±1.5 cm (out and back, turning at the open exit), or add P2-D. With the bucket loader, coverage happens one tile at a time anyway.

Getting to the drop point

Breadth-first search on the tile grid, blocking every known house and street tile, then reuse your lab 3 safest-path moves along the result. Arrive backward for the tilt hopper, with the final line snap taken just before it.

Testing without the robot

utils/dummy.py already fakes the brick. Add a FakeStreet from an ASCII map that answers scan(), and run the explorer on a laptop against ten street shapes. For the real robot, cardboard boxes make a 30 cm street mock-up.

07 · Build order

A sequence that de-risks early

  1. Refactor lab 3 into modules and fix the carry-overs. Add gyro turns and heading hold. Rerun the lab 3 demo to confirm nothing regressed.
  2. Write the explorer against a fake street on a laptop, then on the robot with spin-scan (P1-B) in a cardboard-box street. Save and reload the JSON.
  3. Measure the real pom-poms and the real robot, and put the numbers into the checker. Cut a 1:1 card template of the intake footprint and push it through a box corner by hand.
  4. Build the turret (P1-A) and swap it in. The explorer code doesn't change, only scan().
  5. Prototype the roller on the bench before mounting it. A tube, flaps and a ramp held by hand over 20 pom-poms tells you the lip height and roller speed in ten minutes.
  6. Mount intake and hopper, then tune the corner pre-turn stop on a real corner. Add dumping and drop-point navigation.
  7. Full runs, counted. Log pom-poms collected, contacts and time per run, and change one thing at a time.
08 · Ask the prof

Questions that change the design

  1. Can the street branch or dead-end, and what is its longest length?
  2. Where does the robot start in each phase, and is its starting pose known?
  3. May the phase 1 map be saved to a file and loaded in phase 2?
  4. Do S1 and S2 imply which side of their edge the street is on?
  5. Where are pom-poms placed: anywhere, away from houses, ever on grid lines? What size range?
  6. Does pushing pom-poms a few centimetres while collecting count as dragging? (This decides P2-C.)
  7. Is the drop location a point or a zone, with what tolerance? May the robot dump from a height, or in several trips?
  8. Does the street end open onto the board? Can the robot leave the street there?
  9. Is a light graze of a house a penalty or a fail? Is there a time limit?
  10. Besides paper, cardboard and tape, are rubber bands, string or velcro allowed?
